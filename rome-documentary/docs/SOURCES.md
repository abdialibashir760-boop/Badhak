# Historical source notes

Each claim in the narration, with the evidence behind it and the caveat that applies. **Ancient authors** = primary sources (all written after the events, some centuries later); **modern** = scholarship.

## Cold open — the empire at its height
- *"Territories across three continents"* — provinces in Europe, North Africa and Western Asia (Egypt, Syria, Mesopotamia 116–117 CE). **Modern:** Goldsworthy, *Pax Romana* (2016); Mattingly, *Imperialism, Power, and Identity* (2011).
- *"Tens of millions"* — population estimates for c. 14–150 CE run from ~45 to ~90 million (Scheidel, *Cambridge Companion to the Roman Economy*; Hopkins 1980; Frier 2000). The film gives no single figure on purpose.
- *"About five million square kilometres" at 117 CE* — Taagepera, "Size and Duration of Empires: Growth-Decline Curves, 600 B.C. to 600 A.D.", *Social Science History* 3 (1979) (≈5.0 M km²); Turchin, Adams & Hall (2006) give a similar order of magnitude. Treated as an estimate.

## The Beginning
- *Location (river crossing among low hills, near the sea)* — Livy 1.4–1.7; Cicero, *De Re Publica* 2.5–10 on the advantages of the site; Cornell, *The Beginnings of Rome* (1995).
- *753 BCE, Romulus* — Roman tradition; the date is Varro's calculation (Varronian chronology); Livy 1; Dionysius of Halicarnassus 1.74–75. **Presented as legend.**
- *Villages on the hills by the 8th century BCE* — hut remains and cemeteries on the Palatine and in the Forum valley: Cornell 1995; Forsythe, *A Critical History of Early Rome* (2005); Carandini's claims of an 8th-century city wall are contested and not used.
- *Etruscan neighbours / influence* — Cornell 1995 ch. 4; the later Tarquin kings are traditionally Etruscan.
- *509 BCE, last king expelled → Republic* — Livy 1.59–60, 2.1; Polybius 6.11–14 describes the mature constitution. **Presented as "by tradition"**; the 6th-century chronology is debated.

## The Republic expands
- *Citizen-soldiers; allies as manpower; most of Italy by 264 BCE* — Polybius 2.23–24 (Roman + allied manpower c. 225 BCE); Cornell 1995; Rome had taken Italy south of the Po after the Pyrrhic War (ended 275 BCE) and the fall of Tarentum (272 BCE).
- *First Punic War, 264–241 BCE; fleet built; Sicily won* — Polybius 1.20–63. Sicily becomes the first province after 241 BCE (Sardinia and Corsica 238 BCE; the film's island timings follow these dates).
- *Hannibal across the Alps (218 BCE)* — Polybius 3.47–56; Livy 21.31–38. **The exact pass is disputed** (Col de la Traversette, Col du Clapier, others): the map shows a schematic route.
- *Cannae (216 BCE), one of Rome's worst defeats* — Polybius 3.107–117; Livy 22.44–50. Casualty figures vary widely and are not quoted.
- *Rome refused to surrender* — Livy 22.57–61 (e.g., the senate's refusal to ransom prisoners).
- *Scipio takes the war to Africa; Zama (202 BCE)* — Polybius 15.5–16; Livy 30. **The battlefield location is uncertain**; the film marks it schematically.
- *Carthage destroyed 146 BCE (Third Punic War 149–146)* — Polybius 38–39 (fragments); Appian, *Punic Wars* 8. Corinth also sacked in 146 BCE (not stated in the film). The popular story that Rome "salted the earth" is a modern invention (Ridley, "To Be Taken with a Pinch of Salt", *Classical Philology* 81, 1986) and is **not** repeated.

## Julius Caesar and political turmoil
- *Generals commanding personally loyal armies* — Marius' recruitment reforms and the late-Republic client armies: Keppie, *The Making of the Roman Army* (1984); Syme, *The Roman Revolution* (1939).
- *Gaul, 58–50 BCE* — Caesar, *De Bello Gallico*; Goldsworthy, *Caesar* (2006).
- *Crossing the Rubicon, January 49 BCE, after the Senate demanded he give up his command* — Suetonius, *Divus Iulius* 31–32; Plutarch, *Caesar* 32 and *Pompey* 60; Appian, *Civil Wars* 2.35. The Rubicon marked the boundary of his province.
- *"The die was cast"* — Suetonius gives Latin *iacta alea est*; Plutarch (*Pompey* 60.4) reports Caesar speaking in Greek, quoting Menander, "let the die be cast". **The film says the words are uncertain and shows both wordings on screen.**
- *Defeated Pompey (Pharsalus, 48 BCE) and became dictator* — Caesar, *Bellum Civile* 3; Plutarch, *Caesar* 42–46; dictatorship from 49/48, "perpetual" in early 44 BCE.
- *Assassinated on the Ides of March, 44 BCE* — Suetonius, *Div. Iul.* 80–82; Plutarch, *Caesar* 66; Appian, *Civil Wars* 2.117. The film shows silhouettes only and invents no dialogue.

## The Birth of the Empire
- *Thirteen more years of civil war (44→31 BCE)* — Philippi 42 BCE; Perusine War 41–40; Actium 31 BCE.
- *Actium; Egypt annexed* — Plutarch, *Antony* 61–68; Cassius Dio 50–51; Egypt becomes a Roman province in 30 BCE.
- *27 BCE, the name Augustus* — Augustus, *Res Gestae* 34 ("restored the Republic" to the Senate and people; the title Augustus granted by decree); Cassius Dio 53.16–17; Suetonius, *Aug.* 7. Historians date the start of the Principate to this settlement.
- *Professional army, frontiers, roads, ports, cities* — Augustus' standing legions and the military treasury (aerarium militare, 6 CE): Keppie 1984; Goldsworthy 2016; road/post network: Laurence, *The Roads of Roman Italy* (1999); Kolb, *Transport und Nachrichtentransfer im Römischen Reich* (2000).
- *Pax Romana* — Augustan and Antonine peace as remembered by later authors; Goldsworthy 2016 (the "peace" excludes frontier wars).

## The Legacy
- *Trajan, 117 CE* — Dacian Wars 101–102, 105–106 CE (Dacia a province from 106); Parthian campaign 113–117 CE (Mesopotamia briefly a province in 116–117; given up under Hadrian). Cassius Dio 68; Bennett, *Trajan: Optimus Princeps* (1997). The 117 CE outline on the map is a schematic generalisation; the Hadrian's Wall line (begun 122 CE) is not shown — the northern British frontier is the earlier Tyne–Solway line.
- *Paved roads, aqueducts, frontiers thousands of kilometres long* — Rome had eleven major aqueducts by 226 CE (Frontinus, *De Aquis*, for the first nine); frontier-line length: Whittaker, *Frontiers of the Roman Empire* (1994).
- *Messages took weeks, defence costly, succession often violent* — Kolb 2000 on communication speeds; Goldsworthy, *How Rome Fell* (2009) on succession and cost. The film's "≈ 4,400 km" Britain–Mesopotamia arc is a great-circle calculation between London and Ctesiphon.
- *Influence in law, language, architecture* — e.g., Roman law in modern civil codes; Romance languages; concrete and arch construction (Pantheon).
