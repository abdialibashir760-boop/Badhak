## 1 · THE HOOK — 0:00 → 0:43

**0:00** In 2007, nearly four out of every ten mobile phones sold on Earth carried one name: Nokia. [F11, F12]

**0:07** That year the company shipped four hundred and thirty-seven million handsets, and earned almost eight billion euros in operating profit. [F11, F13]

**0:15** Five years later, a South Korean rival sold more phones. Seven years later, the entire phone business had been sold to Microsoft. [F27, F28]

**0:24** This is not the story of a company that did nothing. It is the story of a company that was winning on every measure it tracked, right up until the measure changed.

**0:36** How does a market leader lose the future it helped build? This is Rise and Ruin.

## 2 · THE EMPIRE — 0:43 → 1:46

**0:43** Nokia began in 1865, as a pulp mill in Finland. [F1] In 1967 it merged with the Finnish Rubber Works and the Finnish Cable Works, forming a sprawling group across forestry, cable, rubber, and electronics. [F2]

**0:56** The pivot came in January 1992, when Jorma Ollila became chief executive. He had run the mobile phone division since 1990, and he had advised against selling it. Instead, he made Nokia a telecommunications company, and divested the units that didn't fit. [F3]

**1:13** The timing mattered. In July 1991, the world's first GSM call was made, on a network built by Nokia. A single digital standard meant a phone could work across borders. Nokia had helped write it. [F4] In 1992, the Nokia 1011 became the first commercially available GSM phone. [F4]

**1:32** The results followed. Operating profit swung from a loss in 1991 to about one billion dollars in 1995, and almost four billion by 1999. [F3] In October 1998, Nokia overtook Motorola as the best-selling mobile phone brand. [F5]


---

## 3 · THE GOLDEN YEARS — 1:46 → 2:47

**1:46** By 2000, Nokia held roughly thirty percent of the market — almost twice its nearest rival. At that peak, it accounted for around four percent of Finland's entire economy, and about seventy percent of the value of the Helsinki stock exchange. [F6, F7]

**2:03** What made Nokia's phones global was how ordinary they were meant to be. Take the Nokia 1100, launched in 2003. It had a built-in flashlight, a dustproof keypad, and the game Snake II. It was designed with developing countries in mind. [F8] Company-linked sources and Reuters put its sales above two hundred and fifty million. That figure is widely repeated, but not independently audited. [F8]

**2:28** By 2007, the peak. Four hundred and thirty-seven million phones shipped. Fifty-one billion euros in net sales. [F11, F13] Nokia estimated its share of the final quarter at forty percent. The research firm Gartner put the full-year figure at thirty-seven point eight. [F12] Either way: more than one in three phones, worldwide.

## 4 · A NEW COMPETITOR ARRIVES — 2:47 → 3:43

**2:48** On January ninth, 2007, Steve Jobs unveiled the iPhone. It reached American stores on June twenty-ninth, from four hundred and ninety-nine dollars. [F14]

**2:56** In its first year it was a rounding error next to Nokia's four hundred and thirty-seven million. But it changed what people expected a phone to be: not a keypad with extras, but a screen with software. **[INTERPRETATION]**

**3:11** Then the platforms multiplied. In November 2007, Google and its partners announced Android. [F15] In 2008, Apple opened an App Store, and in October the first Android phone, the HTC Dream, went on sale in the United States. [F15, F14]

**3:26** And Nokia? Its 2008 net sales were fifty point seven billion euros — almost identical to 2007. [F13] Symbian, its smartphone software, was still the world's most popular smartphone operating system by volume. [F16] On paper, nothing was wrong. That was part of the problem. **[INTERPRETATION]**


---

## 5 · THE PLATFORM PROBLEM — 3:43 → 4:51

**3:43** To see why, you have to understand Symbian. In 2006 it held somewhere between two-thirds and three-quarters of the smartphone market, depending on whose count you trust. [F16] But it had problems that mattered more and more as the industry changed. **[FACT]**

**3:59** Its early development tools were complex, and the kits were expensive, which kept independent developers away. Handset makers used incompatible versions of the interface — S60, UIQ, and MOAP — so an app written for one did not run on another. [F17]

**4:16** Touch came late. Nokia's touch-focused Symbian framework was cancelled as recently as October 2010. [F17] Partners walked away: Motorola, Samsung, LG, and Sony Ericsson all moved toward Android or Windows Phone between 2009 and 2010. [F17]

**4:29** Nokia tried to fix this. It announced the acquisition of Symbian in 2008, and published the source code in February 2010. But the foundation it created disintegrated before that year was out. [F17]

**4:42** And in the final quarter of 2010, the research firm Canalys reported that Android had passed Symbian: about thirty-three million phones, to thirty-one million. [F18]

## 6 · INSIDE THE COMPANY — 4:51 → 6:06

**4:52** Why couldn't a company with this many engineers, and this much profit, respond? The researchers who studied Nokia point away from a single villain.

**5:01** Yves Doz and Keeley Wilson, of the business school INSEAD, spent years studying Nokia's senior managers. Their conclusion: there was no single cause. Nokia, they argue, was in strategic and organisational crisis well before its financial results showed it. [F19] **[INTERPRETATION]**

**5:17** Timo Vuori and Quy Huy interviewed more than fifty Nokia managers. They found something more specific: fear. Top managers feared competitors and shareholders. Middle managers feared their own bosses and peers — not so much losing their jobs as losing status. [F20, F21] **[INTERPRETATION]**

**5:33** The result, they argue, was a flow of information that bent on the way up. Middle managers softened or withheld bad news. Leaders heard optimistic timelines for Symbian that would not be met, and came to believe the company was matching the iPhone when it was not. [F21]

**5:52** That is one scholarly reading, based on interviews from 2005 to 2010. But it fits the shape of the story: a company that could see the numbers — and still could not hear what they meant. **[INTERPRETATION]**

## 7 · THE WRONG TURN? — 6:06 → 7:38

**6:06** In September 2010, Nokia's board replaced its chief executive with Stephen Elop, a Microsoft executive. He was the first non-Finn to lead the company. [F22]

**6:16** Five months later, a memo from Elop leaked. Engadget published it, and later said multiple sources confirmed it was real. It began with a story about a man on a burning oil platform. [F23]

**6:29** "We fell behind, we missed big trends, and we lost time." "The first iPhone shipped in 2007, and we still don't have a product that is close to their experience." And: "The battle of devices has now become a war of ecosystems." [F23]

**6:46** On February eleventh, 2011, in London, Nokia announced its answer: a partnership with Microsoft. Windows Phone would become its primary smartphone platform. Symbian would be phased out. MeeGo, its own next-generation system, was sidelined. [F24]

**7:00** Elop later explained the choice. Android, he said, would have put Nokia on a collision course with Samsung, already ahead on it, and Nokia would have had difficulty differentiating. [F25] **[FACT: stated reasoning]**

**7:11** Was it the wrong turn? Critics point to the sales. Nokia's smartphone shipments fell from twenty-eight million in the final quarter of 2010 to nineteen point six million a year later, and to six point three million by the third quarter of 2012. [F26] They call it the Osborne effect: announcing the future kills demand for the present. Defenders reply that Symbian's share was already falling before the announcement. [F17, F18] **[INTERPRETATION — both views]**


---

## 8 · THE SALE — 7:38 → 8:36

**7:38** In the first quarter of 2012, Samsung overtook Nokia as the world's biggest handset maker. Nokia had held that position since 1998. [F27]

**7:47** On September third, 2013, Microsoft agreed to buy Nokia's devices and services business for three point seven nine billion euros, and to license its patents for another one point six five. Five point four four billion in all. [F28] The deal closed on April twenty-fifth, 2014. Around twenty-five thousand people moved to Microsoft, and Elop went with them. [F28]

**8:10** What Microsoft bought was Nokia's phones. Not Nokia. The company kept its network equipment business, its maps division, and its technology licensing. [F29] That distinction matters, because it is why the story does not end here. **[FACT]**

**8:24** For Microsoft, it ended badly. In July 2015, it took an impairment charge of about seven point six billion dollars on the acquisition, and announced up to seventy-eight hundred job cuts. [F30]


---

## 9 · THE LESSON — 8:36 → 9:43

**8:36** Nokia itself endured. It sold its maps business for two point eight billion euros, and bought the network maker Alcatel-Lucent for fifteen point six billion. [F31] Today it is a network-infrastructure company, with revenue of about twenty billion euros. [F32] The Nokia name returned on phones, built under licence by a company called HMD. [F33]

**8:57** So what does the story teach?

**9:00** First: market share measures yesterday's game. Nokia dominated phones as products. Its rivals were competing on platforms: developers, apps, and services. **[INTERPRETATION]**

**9:08** Second: dominance makes bad news expensive. When success is the culture, honesty about weakness becomes the riskiest thing in the building. **[INTERPRETATION]**

**9:16** Third: every response has a cost. Announcing the future can break the present, and there may have been no clean answer. **[INTERPRETATION]**

**9:25** Nokia sold four hundred and thirty-seven million phones in 2007. It had no single bad day. It had a long run of reasonable decisions, inside a game that had changed. Market dominance is not a position you hold. It is a position you keep earning.
