# Narration script — "How GPS Knows Exactly Where You Are"

**505 words** · target read ≈ 150 wpm · one line per beat (`Bnn`). Each beat is its own audio clip so picture and voice stay in sync (see README → "Adding your voice-over").

## 1 · The hook  _(from 0:02)_

- **B01** Your phone can tell you where you are within seconds.
- **B02** But the satellites helping it do that are thousands of kilometers above your head.
- **B03** And here's the strange part: they don't need to know you exist.

## 2 · What satellites actually send  _(from 0:21)_

- **B04** Around twenty thousand kilometers up, a constellation of GPS satellites circles the Earth, completing two orbits every day.
- **B05** Each satellite does one simple job: it broadcasts. Continuously, toward Earth, it sends out a radio signal.
- **B06** That signal carries the exact time it was sent, plus orbital data that tells receivers where the satellite is.
- **B07** To keep that time, every satellite carries atomic clocks, built for extraordinary precision.
- **B08** Your phone never replies. It only listens. The satellites have no idea who is receiving them.

## 3 · The importance of time  _(from 0:57)_

- **B09** So how does a signal become a distance? Through time.
- **B10** Radio waves travel at the speed of light, almost three hundred thousand kilometers every second.
- **B11** Imagine a satellite about twenty-one thousand kilometers away. Its signal needs roughly seven hundredths of a second to arrive.
- **B12** Your phone compares the time stamped in the message with its own clock. Multiply that delay by the speed of light, and you get a distance.
- **B13** In reality, it's far more delicate. One billionth of a second of error moves the answer by about thirty centimeters. So engineers correct the satellite clocks, even for Einstein's relativity, and the phone solves for its own clock error too.

## 4 · How your phone calculates position  _(from 1:44)_

- **B14** Now comes the clever part: trilateration.
- **B15** One satellite tells your phone it is exactly this far away. So you must be somewhere on the surface of a giant sphere.
- **B16** A second satellite adds another sphere. Where they overlap, you get a circle. A third narrows the circle to just two points, and one of them is far out in space.
- **B17** But there is a catch. Your phone's clock is not atomic, so a tiny clock error makes every distance wrong by the same amount.
- **B18** A fourth satellite fixes that. Four measurements let the phone solve four unknowns: its position in three dimensions, and its clock error.
- **B19** The spheres snap together at a single point. That point is you.

## 5 · Why GPS sometimes fails  _(from 2:34)_

- **B20** Of course, GPS isn't magic. Sometimes it gets things wrong.
- **B21** Between tall buildings, the signals can be blocked completely.
- **B22** Or they bounce off glass and concrete, and arrive late, making a satellite look farther away than it really is.
- **B23** If the satellites you can see are bunched together, the circles of possibility cross at a shallow angle, and the error grows.
- **B24** The atmosphere also slows the signals, and small phone antennas pick up faint signals imperfectly. Some newer phones listen on two frequencies to reduce that atmospheric error.
- **B25** Phones often blend in Wi-Fi and mobile network clues too, but those are separate from GPS itself.

## 6 · The big reveal  _(from 3:19)_

- **B26** Now, pull back.
- **B27** Dozens of satellites, orbiting in six planes, wrap the planet in timing signals, around the clock.
- **B28** GPS is, at its heart, a timing and distance-measurement system.
- **B29** Your phone doesn't need to be watched from space. It listens to signals, measures time, and uses mathematics to work out where it is.
