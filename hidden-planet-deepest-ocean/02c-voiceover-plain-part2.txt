And fish? Not at the very bottom. The deepest fish ever documented live thousands of metres higher up.
The Mariana snailfish is known from about six thousand to eight thousand metres. It is pale, tadpole-like, and reaches about twenty-nine centimetres. In parts of the trench, it appears to be the top predator, feeding on small crustaceans.
In 1960, the crew of the Trieste reported seeing a flatfish on the bottom. Today, some scientists suggest it may have been a sea cucumber instead. Science does not always settle things quickly.
Beyond animals, there are microbes. They are abundant in trench sediments, and they may be the base of the whole system.
How do animals cope with this much pressure? Mostly, by not fighting it. Animals without gas-filled spaces have pressure on the inside equal to the pressure outside.
The challenge is chemistry. Pressure bends proteins out of shape. Many deep-sea animals counter it with small molecules, such as trimethylamine N-oxide, that help proteins keep their form.
In fish, the amount of that molecule rises with depth. Measurements on snailfish from seven thousand metres suggest a limit. Around eight thousand two hundred metres, a fish's body fluids would match the saltiness of seawater.
That is a hypothesis, supported by evidence, and not an unbreakable law. Fish have been filmed deeper than eight thousand metres. But as far as published research shows, no fish have been found in the deepest quarter of the ocean.
Other tricks help, like slower metabolisms and cell membranes built to stay flexible under pressure. In the lab, the amphipod's enzyme even works better at high pressure.
The Challenger Deep is named after the ship HMS Challenger, whose crew first sounded the trench in 1875. In 1951, a second ship named Challenger confirmed its great depth using sonar.
On the twenty-third of January 1960, two men, Jacques Piccard and Don Walsh, descended in the bathyscaphe Trieste. The trip down took almost five hours. They spent about twenty minutes on the bottom, then began the three-hour climb home.
Robots followed. Japan's Kaiko in 1995. The hybrid vehicle Nereus in 2009.
In 2012, filmmaker James Cameron made the first solo dive, in Deepsea Challenger. It reached ten thousand, nine hundred and eight metres.
From 2019, the submersible Limiting Factor made repeated dives. In June 2020, former astronaut Kathy Sullivan became the first woman to reach the Challenger Deep. In November that year, China's Fendouzhe took three scientists to about ten thousand, nine hundred and nine metres.
By mid-2022, twenty-seven people had reached the Challenger Deep.
Even the depth is not settled. The best estimate is ten thousand, nine hundred and thirty-five metres, give or take six. Different surveys give slightly different answers, and measuring through cold, salty, pressurised water is hard.
As of April 2026, only about twenty-nine percent of the global seafloor has been mapped in modern high resolution.
And explorers have directly seen less than one thousandth of one percent of the deep seafloor. That is roughly the area of Rhode Island.
More than four hundred species are known from hadal ecosystems, and new ones are still being found. Others, like that grey sea cucumber, cannot be confirmed without physical samples.
How do living things cope at the extremes, and how far down can animals go? Those questions are still open.
We return now to the darkness. No sunlight. Crushing pressure. Almost no one has been here.
The deepest place on Earth is not empty. It is a world we have only just begun to see.
