Somewhere beneath the western Pacific, there is a place that sunlight has never touched.
It lies at the southern end of the Mariana Trench, almost eleven kilometres down. This is the Challenger Deep, the deepest known point in Earth's ocean.
Here, the water presses down with more than a thousand times the pressure at the surface. It is cold. And it is completely dark.
So what could possibly live here?
The truth is stranger, and more fragile, than any monster we could invent.
Our descent begins in sunlit water. Near the surface, there is enough light for photosynthesis, the energy source behind most of the ocean's food webs.
By two hundred metres, so little light remains that photosynthesis is no longer possible.
Light can still be detected as far as a thousand metres down, but it is faint. Below one thousand metres, sunlight does not reach at all.
At four thousand metres we enter the abyss. At six thousand, the seafloor drops away into the trenches. This is the hadal zone, named after Hades, the Greek god of the underworld.
These trenches are rare. The forty-six known hadal habitats cover less than a quarter of one percent of the seafloor, yet they make up over forty percent of the ocean's depth range.
At the very bottom of the deepest of them, we reach the Challenger Deep: about ten thousand, nine hundred and thirty-five metres. If Mount Everest were placed here, its summit would still be more than two kilometres underwater.
Every ten metres adds roughly one more atmosphere of pressure. At the bottom, that comes to more than one thousand atmospheres, about sixteen thousand pounds on every square inch.
The temperature is just one to four degrees Celsius.
Back above us, in the twilight zone, the last traces of sunlight still reach the animals that live there.
Further down, in total darkness, the only light is the light that animals make themselves. Bioluminescence is a chemical reaction, and it is widespread among deep-sea animals.
These animals live in different depth zones, thousands of metres above the Challenger Deep. As we go deeper, the cast of characters changes.
In the trench, there are no plants. So where does the energy come from?
Mostly, from above. Dead plankton, fragments of animals and other organic matter sink slowly through the water, in what scientists call marine snow.
Much of it is eaten or broken down before it arrives, but trenches act like traps. In the Challenger Deep, scientists measured oxygen use by microbes in the sediment at twice the rate of a nearby site six thousand metres deep.
Earthquakes may help. They can shake material loose from the trench walls and send it sliding to the bottom.
Even sunken plant material may be food. Amphipods caught at nearly eleven thousand metres carry enzymes that can digest cellulose, the main material in wood and plant fibres.
Elsewhere, some seafloor communities run on chemistry instead of light. Around hot vents, bacteria turn chemicals like hydrogen sulphide into energy. These vents are a different environment, but they show that life does not always need the sun.
So, who actually lives at the bottom? Here is what has been documented.
Amphipods. Small, shrimp-like crustaceans. In 2009, Japanese researchers lowered a baited trap into the Challenger Deep and brought up one hundred and eighty-five of them. The species is called Hirondellea gigas.
Single-celled giants called xenophyophores. Some can grow as large as a saucer. They have been seen on the floor of the Challenger Deep, and related single-celled organisms have been found at around ten thousand six hundred metres.
Sea cucumbers. In 2012, camera footage from the Challenger Deep showed a small grey sea cucumber, unlike any scientists had seen. Several of them faced the same direction, as if letting the current carry food to their tentacles.
Worms. In 2009, the robot Nereus spotted a polychaete worm about an inch long. Years before that, Japan's Kaiko robot had recorded a sea cucumber, a scale worm and a shrimp.
