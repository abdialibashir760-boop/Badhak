## 1. The Hook  `[0:00 – 0:42]`

**[0:00]** Somewhere beneath the western Pacific, there is a place that sunlight has never touched.

**[0:06]** It lies at the southern end of the Mariana Trench, almost eleven kilometres down. This is the Challenger Deep, the deepest known point in Earth's ocean.

**[0:18]** Here, the water presses down with more than a thousand times the pressure at the surface. It is cold. And it is completely dark.

**[0:28]** So what could possibly live here?

**[0:32]** The truth is stranger, and more fragile, than any monster we could invent.

**[0:38]** *(silent, 4 s)* Title card: THE HIDDEN PLANET — What Lives in the Deepest Part of the Ocean?


## 2. The Descent  `[0:42 – 2:10]`

**[0:42]** Our descent begins in sunlit water. Near the surface, there is enough light for photosynthesis, the energy source behind most of the ocean's food webs.

**[0:53]** By two hundred metres, so little light remains that photosynthesis is no longer possible.

**[0:59]** Light can still be detected as far as a thousand metres down, but it is faint. Below one thousand metres, sunlight does not reach at all.

**[1:10]** At four thousand metres we enter the abyss. At six thousand, the seafloor drops away into the trenches. This is the hadal zone, named after Hades, the Greek god of the underworld.

**[1:24]** These trenches are rare. The forty-six known hadal habitats cover less than a quarter of one percent of the seafloor, yet they make up over forty percent of the ocean's depth range.

**[1:37]** At the very bottom of the deepest of them, we reach the Challenger Deep: about ten thousand, nine hundred and thirty-five metres. If Mount Everest were placed here, its summit would still be more than two kilometres underwater.

**[1:53]** Every ten metres adds roughly one more atmosphere of pressure. At the bottom, that comes to more than one thousand atmospheres, about sixteen thousand pounds on every square inch.

**[2:06]** The temperature is just one to four degrees Celsius.


## 3. The Twilight Disappears  `[2:10 – 2:40]`

**[2:10]** Back above us, in the twilight zone, the last traces of sunlight still reach the animals that live there.

**[2:19]** Further down, in total darkness, the only light is the light that animals make themselves. Bioluminescence is a chemical reaction, and it is widespread among deep-sea animals.

**[2:30]** These animals live in different depth zones, thousands of metres above the Challenger Deep. As we go deeper, the cast of characters changes.


## 4. Life Without Sunlight  `[2:40 – 3:50]`

**[2:40]** In the trench, there are no plants. So where does the energy come from?

**[2:47]** Mostly, from above. Dead plankton, fragments of animals and other organic matter sink slowly through the water, in what scientists call marine snow.

**[2:57]** Much of it is eaten or broken down before it arrives, but trenches act like traps. In the Challenger Deep, scientists measured oxygen use by microbes in the sediment at twice the rate of a nearby site six thousand metres deep.

**[3:14]** Earthquakes may help. They can shake material loose from the trench walls and send it sliding to the bottom.

**[3:22]** Even sunken plant material may be food. Amphipods caught at nearly eleven thousand metres carry enzymes that can digest cellulose, the main material in wood and plant fibres.

**[3:34]** Elsewhere, some seafloor communities run on chemistry instead of light. Around hot vents, bacteria turn chemicals like hydrogen sulphide into energy. These vents are a different environment, but they show that life does not always need the sun.


## 5. Strange Survivors  `[3:50 – 5:42]`

**[3:50]** So, who actually lives at the bottom? Here is what has been documented.

**[3:56]** Amphipods. Small, shrimp-like crustaceans. In 2009, Japanese researchers lowered a baited trap into the Challenger Deep and brought up one hundred and eighty-five of them. The species is called Hirondellea gigas.

**[4:10]** Single-celled giants called xenophyophores. Some can grow as large as a saucer. They have been seen on the floor of the Challenger Deep, and related single-celled organisms have been found at around ten thousand six hundred metres.

**[4:25]** Sea cucumbers. In 2012, camera footage from the Challenger Deep showed a small grey sea cucumber, unlike any scientists had seen. Several of them faced the same direction, as if letting the current carry food to their tentacles.

**[4:41]** Worms. In 2009, the robot Nereus spotted a polychaete worm about an inch long. Years before that, Japan's Kaiko robot had recorded a sea cucumber, a scale worm and a shrimp.

**[4:54]** And fish? Not at the very bottom. The deepest fish ever documented live thousands of metres higher up.

**[5:02]** The Mariana snailfish is known from about six thousand to eight thousand metres. It is pale, tadpole-like, and reaches about twenty-nine centimetres. In parts of the trench, it appears to be the top predator, feeding on small crustaceans.

**[5:18]** In 1960, the crew of the Trieste reported seeing a flatfish on the bottom. Today, some scientists suggest it may have been a sea cucumber instead. Science does not always settle things quickly.

**[5:32]** Beyond animals, there are microbes. They are abundant in trench sediments, and they may be the base of the whole system.


## 6. The Pressure Problem  `[5:42 – 6:49]`

**[5:42]** How do animals cope with this much pressure? Mostly, by not fighting it. Animals without gas-filled spaces have pressure on the inside equal to the pressure outside.

**[5:53]** The challenge is chemistry. Pressure bends proteins out of shape. Many deep-sea animals counter it with small molecules, such as trimethylamine N-oxide, that help proteins keep their form.

**[6:05]** In fish, the amount of that molecule rises with depth. Measurements on snailfish from seven thousand metres suggest a limit. Around eight thousand two hundred metres, a fish's body fluids would match the saltiness of seawater.

**[6:20]** That is a hypothesis, supported by evidence, and not an unbreakable law. Fish have been filmed deeper than eight thousand metres. But as far as published research shows, no fish have been found in the deepest quarter of the ocean.

**[6:37]** Other tricks help, like slower metabolisms and cell membranes built to stay flexible under pressure. In the lab, the amphipod's enzyme even works better at high pressure.


## 7. Human Exploration  `[6:49 – 7:56]`

**[6:49]** The Challenger Deep is named after the ship HMS Challenger, whose crew first sounded the trench in 1875. In 1951, a second ship named Challenger confirmed its great depth using sonar.

**[7:02]** On the twenty-third of January 1960, two men, Jacques Piccard and Don Walsh, descended in the bathyscaphe Trieste. The trip down took almost five hours. They spent about twenty minutes on the bottom, then began the three-hour climb home.

**[7:18]** Robots followed. Japan's Kaiko in 1995. The hybrid vehicle Nereus in 2009.

**[7:24]** In 2012, filmmaker James Cameron made the first solo dive, in Deepsea Challenger. It reached ten thousand, nine hundred and eight metres.

**[7:34]** From 2019, the submersible Limiting Factor made repeated dives. In June 2020, former astronaut Kathy Sullivan became the first woman to reach the Challenger Deep. In November that year, China's Fendouzhe took three scientists to about ten thousand, nine hundred and nine metres.

**[7:52]** By mid-2022, twenty-seven people had reached the Challenger Deep.


## 8. What Remains Unknown  `[7:56 – 8:51]`

**[7:56]** Even the depth is not settled. The best estimate is ten thousand, nine hundred and thirty-five metres, give or take six. Different surveys give slightly different answers, and measuring through cold, salty, pressurised water is hard.

**[8:11]** As of April 2026, only about twenty-nine percent of the global seafloor has been mapped in modern high resolution.

**[8:20]** And explorers have directly seen less than one thousandth of one percent of the deep seafloor. That is roughly the area of Rhode Island.

**[8:30]** More than four hundred species are known from hadal ecosystems, and new ones are still being found. Others, like that grey sea cucumber, cannot be confirmed without physical samples.

**[8:42]** How do living things cope at the extremes, and how far down can animals go? Those questions are still open.


## 9. Back to the Dark  `[8:51 – 9:15]`

**[8:51]** We return now to the darkness. No sunlight. Crushing pressure. Almost no one has been here.

**[8:59]** The deepest place on Earth is not empty. It is a world we have only just begun to see.
