# Why Time Speeds Up As You Get Older: voice-over script

Length: about 3:20 at a calm pace (~145 wpm, 480 words). `[pause]` = 1 second of silence. Scene headings are for the animation only; do not read them.

## 1. The question (0:00)
Remember summers as a kid? [pause] They felt endless. A single afternoon could hold an entire adventure.

Now? [pause] You blink, and it's December. Again.

Almost everyone feels this. And it is not just in your head. Well, it *is* in your head. But there are real reasons why.

## 2. The simple theory (0:25)
The oldest explanation is about proportion. When you are ten, one year is ten percent of your whole life. When you are fifty, one year is just two percent.

A smaller slice *feels* shorter. [pause]

It is a neat idea. But scientists admit it cannot be the whole story. Because it doesn't explain why one boring week can drag, while a busy month vanishes.

## 3. The real clue: memory (1:00)
Here is the better clue. Your brain doesn't measure time with a clock. [pause] It measures time by *what it remembers*.

When something is new, your brain pays close attention, and stores a lot of detail. First day of school. First trip abroad. First time you were in love. Looking back, those periods feel long, because they are full of memories.

But routine is different. Same commute. Same coffee. Same screen. Your brain recognises the pattern and quietly saves almost nothing. [pause]

So when you look back at a year of routine, there is very little to count. And a year with little to count feels short.

## 4. The holiday paradox (1:55)
Psychologists have a name for a strange effect. A holiday flies by while you are living it. But afterwards, it feels *long*, because it was packed with new memories.

And a dull stretch is the opposite. Slow while you live it. Over in a blink when you look back.

Which means time is not speeding up. [pause] Your *memories* of it are getting thinner.

## 5. Why it gets worse with age (2:30)
Childhood is full of firsts. Adult life, for most of us, is full of repeats. Work, home, sleep, repeat. Fewer firsts means fewer markers, and the years blur together.

Researchers also note that attention matters. When we are distracted, we hardly notice the moments passing. [pause] And hours of scrolling leave almost no memory behind.

## 6. What you can do (2:55)
The good news: you can slow it down.

Do something new. A new route, a new skill, a new city. [pause] Even small firsts count.

Pay attention on purpose. Put the phone down for one meal, and actually taste it.

And mark your life. Take a photo, write a line each day. Give your brain something to count.

## 7. Close (3:15)
You can't add hours to the day. But you can fill them with moments worth remembering.

[pause]

Because a long life is not the one with the most years. [pause] It is the one with the most *memories*.

---
Notes: the proportionality idea (a year feels shorter as it becomes a smaller share of your life) is a popular theory, not proven. The memory-density explanation and the "holiday paradox" (a term popularised by psychologist Claudia Hammond) are better supported, but researchers still debate the full cause. The script says so on purpose.
